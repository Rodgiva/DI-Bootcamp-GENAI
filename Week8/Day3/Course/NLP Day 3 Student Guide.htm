RAG on real documents, and how to check it

Everything so far worked on sentences of about two hundred characters. Today's documents average ten thousand. You run yesterday's pipeline unchanged — and it fails without a single error message.

By the end of today you can:

  1. Explain an embedding model's reading window, and why long documents fail silently.
  2. Chunk documents, and choose a chunk size on purpose rather than by default.
  3. Rebuild the whole pipeline in LangChain, and say what LangChain actually is.
  4. Read the prompt the framework wrote for you without asking.
  5. Treat k as a token budget instead of a dial marked "more is better".
  6. Tell a retrieval failure from a model failure by reading the retrieved chunks.
  7. Measure whether an answer actually came from your documents.

Why it matters. Long, messy documents are what real corpora look like, and a RAG system that is quietly broken looks exactly like one that works. The habits you build today — read the chunks, check the grounding — are the only things that tell them apart.

Where today sits in the week

The one idea: everything in the first two days ran on text about two hundred characters long. Real documents are ten thousand. Today you run yesterday's pipeline, completely unchanged, on real documents — and it fails without a single error message. The day is about why, and what you do about it.

Three jobs in a line:

  1. Make long documents fit. Every embedding model has a reading window, and text past the end of it is silently ignored. So you cut documents into chunks — and you choose the size on purpose instead of accepting a default.
  2. Rebuild it in a framework. LangChain does the same work in a fifth of the code, and hides the prompt while doing it. You will read that hidden prompt out loud.
  3. Diagnose it. k is a token budget, not a dial marked "more is better". When an answer is wrong you read the retrieved chunks before blaming the model. And you measure whether the answer actually came from your documents at all.

What today is not. There is no new retrieval maths. The embeddings, the index and the cosine similarity are all yesterday's, untouched. The new variable is the shape of the documents — and that turns out to be most of the work in a real system.

The grounding score at the end is one measurement, not evaluation. It answers "did this answer come from the text?" and nothing else. The full toolkit is tomorrow.

STEP 5

Find out why — the reading window

An embedding model doesn't read as much text as you give it. It has a fixed window, and it never tells you when you go over.

The library

Here is what she has been doing. She only ever reads the first page. She writes her summary from that, files it, and moves on.

And these manuals all open with the same licence page. So her summary of manual 7 and her summary of manual 183 are nearly the same summary — because she read nearly the same page. The ranking was never about the content.

How much did it actually read?

The thing to remember from today

Every embedding model has a reading window. Find out what yours is before you store anything. Going over doesn't warn you, doesn't error, and doesn't even slow down.

  2. Why is this worse than an error message?
    Show the answer

  3. On a new corpus, how would you check for this yourself in two lines?
    Show the answer

STEP 6

Cut the documents into readable pieces

If the model can only read a thousand characters at a time, give it a thousand characters at a time. The pieces are called chunks.

The library

So stop handing her whole manuals. Tear each one into sections short enough that she reads to the end, and let her summarise each section on its own.

Now a summary describes something she actually read.

What the separators mean

It splits on blank lines first, then single newlines, then spaces, then individual characters — only dropping to a cruder cut when a piece is still too big. That's why it's called recursive. The overlap repeats a bit of text between neighbours so a sentence on a boundary survives whole somewhere.

STEP 7

Choosing a chunk size is a trade

Chunk size is a trade, not a setting with a right answer. Run three settings side by side and pick one on purpose.

The library

How big a section? Too small and the sentence you need is separated from the paragraph that explains it. Too big and she is back to reading only the opening.

And the catch: you are measuring sections in pages; she measures them in how much she can read. They are not the same unit, and assuming they are is how you end up just over the line without noticing.

Three sizes compared

Yes, really — and this is the lesson

We spent ten minutes fixing silent truncation and the setting we picked has an average a couple of tokens over the line. It still works well, because the useful sentence is rarely at the very end of a chunk.

But an average is the wrong number to decide on, and this is the part worth taking with you. A mean of 258 does not tell you how many chunks are over 256 — that could be most of them, or a few long ones dragging the mean up. Measure the share that actually exceeds the limit, and the worst case, with the cell below.

Then: "it still works" is not the same as "it's correct." If you were building this for real you'd use 500. Choosing to live with a known compromise is fine. Not knowing you made one is not.

The check the average could not give you
LIMIT = embedder.max_seq_length          # ask the model, do not hardcode 256

print(f"{'chunk_size':>11} {'median':>8} {'max':>7}   over the {LIMIT}-token limit")
print("=" * 56)

    sp = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(chunk_size=size, chunk_overlap=overlap)
    pieces = [p for t in texts for p in sp.split_text(t)]         # texts, one doc at a time

Read the new numbers, not the old ones

The percentage is the answer, and the maximum tells you how much the worst chunk loses. chunk_size is counted in characters; the model's window is counted in tokens. The two are related by roughly four characters per token in English prose — a rule of thumb, never a conversion. This cell is how you stop guessing.

What "stuff" means

chain_type="stuff" = paste every retrieved chunk into one prompt. One model call, fast. "refine" = answer from chunk 1, then revise with chunk 2, and so on — one call per chunk, slow but nothing gets dropped. That's the one your daily challenge uses.

STEP 11

Read the sources before you trust the answer

A RAG system has two things that can go wrong and the output looks the same either way. The only way to tell them apart is to read the chunks.

The library

The answer sounds right. Ask which pages it came from, and go and read them.

Because two completely different failures produce an answer that looks identical: the right pages were never found, or the right pages were found and misread. From the answer alone you cannot tell which. From the pages you can, in about ten seconds.

Two questions, same system
def ask_and_show(question, k=2):
    out = RetrievalQA.from_chain_type(
        llm=llm, chain_type="stuff",
        retriever=db.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": k}),
        return_source_documents=True,

    print("   retrieved from:")
    for d in out["source_documents"]:
        print(f"      {d.metadata['source'].split('/')[-1]}")
    print()
    return out

STEP 12

Did it answer from our text, or invent it?

Now we find out whether that “say you don't know” instruction means anything. The check is crude: count how many words of the answer appear in the text we retrieved.

The library

Last question: did she answer from the pages, or from something she half-remembered?

Our check is crude — how many of her words appear on the pages at all. That catches an answer invented out of nothing. It does not catch an answer that borrows the right vocabulary and still says something the pages never said. Knowing which of those you have caught is the whole of tomorrow.

Grounding check
def grounded_fraction(answer, docs):
    context = " ".join(d.page_content for d in docs).lower()
    words = re.findall(r"[a-z]{4,}", answer.lower())
    if not words:
        return 0.0, 0, 0
    hits = sum(w in context for w in words)
    return hits / len(words), hits, len(words)

for question in [QUESTION, "What is the capital city of Uruguay?"]:
    out = RetrievalQA.from_chain_type(
        llm=llm, chain_type="stuff",
        retriever=db.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 2}),
        return_source_documents=True,
    ).invoke({"query": question})
    share, hits, total = grounded_fraction(out["result"], out["source_documents"])
    print(f"Q: {question}")

The conclusion of the whole week

A prompt instruction is not a guarantee. This model ignored it on this question — which is one example, not a law about every small model. But it is enough to kill the assumption: you cannot ship "I told it to say I don't know" as a safety measure.

What you need instead is a count: how often does your system abstain when the retrieved context cannot answer? That is a measurement, and measuring it is what tomorrow is about.

STEP 13

The two windows — the thing to take away

Today you met two numbers that look the same and behave completely differently. Confusing them is the most common way people break a RAG system.

The library — and where it breaks

Two limits, two different people. How much of each manual the summariser reads before she files it — exceed that and text is dropped silently, at filing time, and you find out months later. And how much the answerer can hold in front of her at once — exceed that and you simply get a worse answer.

And here is where the whole analogy finally breaks. A librarian who had only read the first page would tell you so. She would say “I only skimmed it.” The model never says that. It has no idea it was handed half a document, and it will answer in exactly the same confident voice either way.

That is the entire reason today existed. Not chunking, not LangChain — the fact that nothing in this system will ever tell you it did not read the page.

And the lesson that outlives all the libraries

This whole ecosystem breaks compatibility every few months — that's why Step 1 has two pins in it. When an example from a tutorial fails on an import or a task name, check the version before you doubt the code. It is almost never a logic error.

Two things to be able to say out loud

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation — generation whose prompt has been augmented with retrieved text. In practice it is three steps, and they are the three boxes you built today: retrieve the relevant chunks, augment the prompt with them, generate the answer from that prompt.

RAG is not fine-tuning. Fine-tuning changes what the model knows and costs a training run; RAG changes what the model can see and costs a database lookup. If your facts change weekly, you want RAG.

Check you understood

The last three. The first one is the thing most likely to catch you in your own project.

  1. Name the two windows, and say what each one limits.
    Show the answer

    The embedding model’s window limits how much of a document becomes a vector at all — exceed it and text is silently dropped at indexing time. The language model’s context window limits how much retrieved text it can read while answering. They are different sizes, they fail at different moments, and confusing them is the commonest way people break a RAG system.

  2. ‘k is a budget.’ A budget of what, exactly?
    Show the answer

    Of the language model’s context window. Every extra chunk you retrieve is more text it has to read. Raising k improves your chance of including the right chunk, and past a point it buries that chunk among irrelevant ones or pushes it out of the window entirely.

  3. The groundedness check counts how many of the answer’s words appear in the retrieved text. Give one way it is wrong in each direction.
    Show the answer

    False alarm: a perfectly correct answer, written in the model’s own words rather than the document’s, scores low. False pass: a model can echo plenty of retrieved vocabulary while still asserting something the text never said. It is a crude smoke alarm, not a proof.

The golden rule, one more time

Cells remember what earlier cells did. Skip one, or restart, and everything after it stops working. When in doubt: restart and run them all from the top.
