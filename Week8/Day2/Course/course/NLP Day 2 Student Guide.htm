Search that understands what you meant

Yesterday's search engine scored 0.000 on a reworded question. Today you fix it in two lines — and then build everything that has to exist around that fix before it is a system anyone could use.

By the end of today you can:

  1. Turn a sentence into one vector that stands for its meaning, not its words.
  2. Say where that loses to yesterday's method, and build hybrid search — the two halves of Day 1 and Day 2, merged.
  3. Measure what exact search costs, then make it faster with IVF and HNSW and measure what the speed cost you.
  4. Search ten thousand documents with a real index (FAISS), and say what it cost.
  5. Store text, labels and filters together in a vector database (Chroma).
  6. Build RAG: search first, then let a language model answer from what you found.
  7. Reorder the results with a reranker and watch the answer change.

Why it matters. RAG is the standard way to make a language model answer from your documents instead of its memory. Everything you build today is the pipeline that sits behind almost every AI product that cites a source.

Where today sits in the week

The one idea: yesterday the numbers came from counting words. Today they come from a model that has already read billions of words, so two pieces of text about the same thing get similar numbers even when they share none of the same words. That change is about two lines of code. The rest of the day is everything that has to exist around it before it is a system anyone could use.

Three jobs in a line:

  1. Make the numbers mean something. A sentence goes in, one vector comes out, and it stands for the meaning rather than the spelling.
  2. Store and search them at scale. Ten documents is a for loop; ten thousand needs an index. FAISS for speed, Chroma for keeping text, labels and filters together.
  3. Use them. RAG — search first, then let a language model answer from what you found. Then reranking, which reorders the results and changes the answer.

What today is not. The documents are still short — single sentences and small paragraphs. A lot of today works partly because of that, and tomorrow is what happens when it stops being true.

There is also still no real evaluation: you judge answers by eye. Day 4 turns that into a measurement.

And the thing to notice: today is where the two halves finally join. Yesterday's word matching and today's meaning matching get combined into hybrid search. Neither half wins on its own — that is why both days exist.

STEP 4

The fix — two lines

Where we are. Yesterday’s search scored 0.000 on a reworded question. Nothing is fixed yet. This step fixes it — in two lines.

Yesterday we counted words. Now we make one number for the whole sentence, based on what it's about — which is why the paraphrase that scored 0.000 yesterday now works. How: we hand the sentence to a model that has already read billions of sentences, and keep the 384 numbers it hands back.

The library — start here

For the next two hours we are building a library. Not the books — the system that finds things in them. Every step today is one more thing that library needs.

Right now you have hired a librarian who has read every book in the building and remembers a one-sentence summary of each. Ask for the book about a whale and obsession and she walks straight to it. She never needed your words. She needed your meaning.

In plain words

Where the model came from

Two words you'll hear a lot

Sparse = yesterday's method (one slot per word, nearly all zeros). Dense = today's (384 slots, all filled). Production systems run both and merge the results — that's called hybrid search.

STEP 6

Keep both — hybrid search

Where we are. Meaning search works; exact codes are broken. This step fixes exact codes without giving up meaning.

Step 5 left you with two rankings that disagree. Neither is right on its own, so you keep both and merge them. This is the thing Day 1 promised you would build — the moment the two halves of search finally join.

The library

So you keep the librarian and the old card index. She is useless with numbers; the index is useless with ideas. Ask both, and trust whatever they both put near the top.

Neither one got promoted. You just stopped relying on either one alone.

Reciprocal rank fusion — merge by position, not by score
def rank_of(scores):
    """Position of every document in a ranking: 1 = best."""
    order = np.argsort(scores)[::-1]
    ranks = np.empty(len(scores), dtype=int)
    ranks[order] = np.arange(1, len(scores) + 1)
    return ranks

K = 60   # the standard constant; it damps the weight of low positions
rrf = 1 / (K + rank_of(dense)) + 1 / (K + rank_of(kws))

print(f"{'MEANING':>8} {'KEYWORD':>8} {'HYBRID':>7}   document")
print("=" * 88)
for i in np.argsort(rrf)[::-1]:
    print(f"{rank_of(dense)[i]:>8} {rank_of(kws)[i]:>8} {rank_of(rrf)[i]:>7}   {TICKETS[i][:58]}")

print(f"\n-> Hybrid top hit: {TICKETS[int(np.argmax(rrf))]}")
What the code does
  1. rank_of(scores) — turns a list of scores into positions: 1 for the best document, 2 for the next, and so on. The two lines with order are just a way of inverting a sort.
  2. K = 60 — the standard constant from the original paper. It stops the top position from dominating everything: with K=60, rank 1 scores 1/61 and rank 2 scores 1/62, which are close together. A smaller K makes first place count for much more.
  3. 1/(K + rank) + 1/(K + rank) — add up what each method thinks. A document only wins if both methods like it, or one of them loves it.
  4. the loop — prints all five tickets with all three rankings side by side, so you can see what moved.

The right invoice is now rank 1. Meaning-search had it second; keyword-search had it first but with a tiny raw score. Neither was trustworthy alone, and merging by position fixed it.
Why merge positions instead of scores

The two scores are not on the same scale and never will be. Meaning-similarity runs roughly 0.3–0.9 for almost anything; TF-IDF similarity is near zero most of the time and spikes when a rare word matches. Adding them together would let whichever method happens to produce bigger numbers win every time.

Positions have no such problem. "Second place" means the same thing in both lists. That is the whole trick, and it is why this three-line function is what ships: Elasticsearch, Azure AI Search and OpenSearch all implement reciprocal rank fusion. How far ahead hybrid finishes depends entirely on the data and the questions — which is why you measure it on your own search task rather than trusting a number from someone else's.

STEP 8

What exact search costs

Where we are. 10,000 documents, and search still works. We just don’t know what it costs. This step measures it.

You now have 10,000 vectors and a question. The obvious way to search is to compare the question against every single article — that is exact k-nearest-neighbour search, and it is what we have been doing all along without naming it. Before improving it, measure it.

The library

Your method so far is: pick up every book, one at a time, decide, put it down. Fine for a shelf.

So time one shelf, multiply by the number of shelves in the building — and that number is why the rest of today exists.

Time it, then do the arithmetic for real corpus sizes
def knn_search(question, k=5):
    """Exact search: compare the question against every article."""
    q = embedder.encode([question], normalize_embeddings=True).astype('float32')
    scores = (news_vectors @ q.T).ravel()
    top = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:k]
    return [(int(i), float(scores[i])) for i in top]

QUESTION = "How many medals did Michael Phelps win at the Athens Olympics?"
q_vec = embedder.encode([QUESTION], normalize_embeddings=True).astype('float32')

runs = 20
start = time.time()
for _ in range(runs):
    _ = news_vectors @ q_vec.T
per_query_ms = (time.time() - start) / runs * 1000

print(f"{'documents':>14}  {'time per question':>18}  {'memory for the vectors':>24}")
print("=" * 62)
for n in [len(news), 1_000_000, 10_000_000, 100_000_000]:
    ms = per_query_ms * n / len(news)
    gb = n * 384 * 4 / 1e9
    measured = "   <- measured" if n == len(news) else ""
    shown = f"{ms:,.1f} ms" if ms < 1000 else f"{ms/1000:,.1f} s"
    ram = f"{gb*1000:,.0f} MB" if gb < 1 else f"{gb:,.1f} GB"
    print(f"{n:>14,}  {shown:>18}  {ram:>24}{measured}")

print("\nAnd the answers are exact — it looked at every article:")
for i, sc in knn_search(QUESTION, k=3):
    print(f"   {sc:.3f}  {news.text[i][:88]}")
What the code does
  1. news_vectors @ q.T — one matrix multiply compares the question against all 10,000 articles at once. Because the vectors are normalised, this is cosine similarity.
  2. runs = 20 and the loop — time it twenty times and average, because a single timing on a shared machine is noise.
  3. the for n in [...] loop — no new measurement happens here. It takes the one measured time and scales it linearly, because exact search really is linear: twice the documents, twice the work.
  4. n * 384 * 4 / 1e9 — memory. 384 numbers per vector, 4 bytes per number (float32).

STEP 9

Put the vectors in a real index

Where we are. Brute force works, but the cost grows in a straight line. This step replaces it with a real index.

Comparing against every document works, but the cost grows in a straight line. FAISS is a library built for exactly that job.

The library

You still check every book — but now on a conveyor belt instead of carrying them by hand. Same books, same answer, a fraction of the time.

Nothing clever has happened yet. The job is just being done properly.

FAISS
QUESTION = "How many medals did Michael Phelps win at the Athens Olympics?"

index = faiss.IndexIDMap(faiss.IndexFlatIP(384))
index.add_with_ids(news_vectors, news["id"].to_numpy().astype('int64'))

def faiss_search(question, k=5):
    q = embedder.encode([question], normalize_embeddings=True).astype('float32')
    scores, ids = index.search(q, k)
    return list(zip(ids[0].tolist(), scores[0].tolist()))

for i, s in faiss_search(QUESTION, k=3):
    print(f"   {s:.3f}  [{i}] {news.text[i][:72]}")

Three things that will catch you

1. FAISS wants float32. 2. The index size must be 384 — that's what this model outputs. 3. faiss.normalize_L2(x) changes x and returns nothing; writing x = faiss.normalize_L2(x) destroys your vectors.

This index is still exact

It compares your question against every vector, so it is correct every time — just organised and faster than doing it by hand. Step 10 is where you stop looking at everything, and where you find out what that costs.

STEP 10

Stop looking at everything — and measure what that costs

Where we are. The index is faster, but still reads every vector. This step skips most of them — and measures what you lose.

Exact search reads all 10,000 vectors for every question. Approximate search deliberately skips most of them, and accepts that it might miss the best document. That trade is the entire field of vector search — and it is only a good trade if you measure what you lost.

The library

Now you stop visiting every aisle and go only to the three sections most likely to hold it. Nearly always right — and occasionally the book was shelved oddly and you walk straight past it.

And the only reason you know how often that happens is that you already did it the slow way once. That is all recall is measuring: your shortcut against your own exhaustive search.

IVF — group the vectors into clusters, read only a few
PROBE_QUESTIONS = [          # measure on several questions, not one
    "How many gold medals did Michael Phelps win at the Athens Olympics?",
    "oil prices and the economy", "new Microsoft software release",
    "election results in Europe", "stock market falls",
    "space telescope discovery", "Apple computer sales",
    "football transfer news", "hurricane damage in Florida",
    "peace talks in the Middle East", "internet security flaw", "airline bankruptcy",
]
probe_vectors = embedder.encode(PROBE_QUESTIONS, normalize_embeddings=True).astype('float32')

def timed_search(idx, k=10, runs=20):
    """Average milliseconds per question, and the ids each question got back."""
    start = time.time()
    for _ in range(runs):
        _, ids = idx.search(probe_vectors, k)
    ms = (time.time() - start) / runs / len(PROBE_QUESTIONS) * 1000
    return ms, ids

flat = faiss.IndexFlatIP(384)
flat.add(news_vectors)
exact_ms, exact_ids = timed_search(flat)      # the answer key

NLIST = 200
ivf = faiss.IndexIVFFlat(faiss.IndexFlatIP(384), 384, NLIST, faiss.METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT)
ivf.train(news_vectors)                       # the clustering step — only IVF needs this
ivf.add(news_vectors)

print(f"{'nprobe':>7}  {'clusters read':>14}  {'ms per question':>16}  {'recall@10':>10}")
print("=" * 56)
for nprobe in [1, 2, 5, 10, 25, NLIST]:
    ivf.nprobe = nprobe
    ms, ids = timed_search(ivf)
    recall = np.mean([len(set(ids[j]) & set(exact_ids[j])) / 10
                      for j in range(len(PROBE_QUESTIONS))])
    tail = "   <- this IS exact search" if nprobe == NLIST else ""
    print(f"{nprobe:>7}  {nprobe/NLIST:>13.1%}  {ms:>16.3f}  {recall:>10.2f}{tail}")

print(f"\nexact search: {exact_ms:.3f} ms per question, recall 1.00 by definition")
What the code does
  1. PROBE_QUESTIONS — twelve questions, not one. A single question can get lucky; an average over twelve cannot.
  2. flat and exact_ids — build the exact index first and keep its answers. Everything else is scored against these.
  3. IndexIVFFlat(..., NLIST, ...) — IVF sorts the 10,000 vectors into 200 clusters. A question then only has to be compared against the vectors in the nearest few clusters.
  4. ivf.train(news_vectors) — works out where the 200 clusters are. Only IVF needs a training step, and it needs it before you add anything.
  5. ivf.nprobe — how many clusters to actually read. This is the dial: 1 is fastest and blindest, 200 reads everything.
  6. recall — of the 10 results exact search returned, how many did the fast index also return? set(ids) & set(exact_ids) is the overlap.

What recall here does and does not tell you

Recall@10 measures agreement with exact search — nothing more. It is not a measure of whether the results are any good. If exact search ranks the wrong article first, an approximate index that reproduces that ranking perfectly scores recall 1.00 and is just as wrong, just faster.

So this table answers one question only: how much did the shortcut change the answers? Whether those answers were right in the first place is a different measurement — and that is Day 4.

IVF is the easy one to explain. The one you will actually meet works the opposite way: it divides the space into nothing at all. Instead, every vector keeps a short list of its nearest neighbours — and you find things by walking from one to the next.

How HNSW actually searches

Give every vector a contacts list: the 32 others closest to it. No drawers, no regions, no training step — just 10,000 vectors, each knowing 32 neighbours.

To search, start anywhere and repeat one move: “of my 32 neighbours, which is closest to the question? Go there.” Stop when no neighbour is closer than where you are standing. Each hop takes a big bite out of the remaining distance, so you arrive in about a dozen hops instead of 10,000 comparisons. You never look at the data as a whole — you just keep stepping towards the question.

And the H is for Hierarchical — the actual trick

The graph is built in layers. The top layer holds very few vectors with very long links, so two or three hops cross the whole space. Each layer down holds more vectors with shorter links. The bottom layer holds everything, with short local links.

You start at the top, travel far fast, drop a layer, refine, drop again, and do the fine detail at the bottom. It is how you reach a street address in another country: fly to the nearest big airport, take a train to the nearest town, walk the last few streets. Three zoom levels, each one closer — and nobody ever consults a map of every address.

The full name is Hierarchical Navigable Small World. “Small world” is the six-degrees-of-separation idea: you can reach a stranger by repeatedly asking “who do you know who is closer to this person?” — because the network was built so a handful of hops reaches anyone.

HNSW — a graph instead of clusters. This is what vector databases run.
hnsw = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(384, 32, faiss.METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT)  # 32 = links per vector

What the code does
  1. IndexHNSWFlat(384, 32, ...) — 32 is the size of each vector’s contacts list. More links mean better paths through the graph and more memory, and it is fixed when the index is built — unlike efSearch, you cannot change it afterwards. Note there is no training step: IVF had to work out where its clusters were; HNSW just starts linking.
  2. hnsw.efSearch — how many candidate paths to keep open while walking. Not one path — a handful. Walking greedily can strand you somewhere no neighbour is closer but which is not actually the best vector, just the bottom of a small dip. Keeping 16 candidates alive lets you back out of that dead end. This is HNSW’s nprobe: the same “how thorough am I being” dial, which is why the two tables have the same shape.
  3. timed_search(hnsw) — the same timing and the same recall check as IVF, so the two tables are directly comparable.

Against exact search at 3.583 ms, that is roughly fifty times faster at full recall on this corpus. HNSW is the default index inside Chroma, Pinecone, Weaviate and Qdrant — so this is what you will be running in production whether or not you ever type its name.
The dial does nothing here — and that is the point

Recall is already 1.00 at the cheapest setting, so turning efSearch up just costs time and buys nothing. Reasonable question: why does the parameter exist?

Because 10,000 vectors is far too small to make HNSW struggle. The graph is so well connected at this size that the laziest possible walk already finds everything. At ten million vectors that column moves plenty.

So the two tables do different jobs. The IVF table showed you the trade; this one shows you the destination — and it only looks this good because our corpus is small. Do not read 1.00 as a promise. Read it as the reason you measure.

What HNSW costs

It is not free. The graph itself has to be stored, so it uses more memory than IVF, and building it gets slow as the corpus grows — 2.1 seconds here is nothing, but at ten million vectors it is a real job. Deleting vectors is awkward too, which is why many systems rebuild instead of deleting.

That is the bargain: the best speed-to-recall curve in the business, paid for in memory and build time. For a search workload that is the right side of the trade, which is exactly why every vector database reached for it.

The rule to write down

Build the exact index first and keep it as your answer key. Speed is bought with recall. If you did not measure recall, you do not know what you bought.

And the sizing advice that saves people from infrastructure they do not need: under about a hundred thousand vectors, just use exact search. No tuning, no missed documents, no surprises. Most projects never exceed that.

Filtering afterwards is not filtering

Think of a library search that returns the ten most relevant books and then you check which are in English. If all ten happen to be in German, you get nothing — even though the library has a thousand relevant English books on the shelf.

STEP 15

Fix the order, and the answer changes

Where we are. RAG works, but it is only as good as the order of what you retrieved. This step reorders it.

The search found the right articles; the model read the top of the list, and the top was a mid-tournament report. Retrieval wasn't bad — the order was.

The library — and where it breaks

You handed over twenty pages and they answered from whichever was on top — which was a mid-tournament report, so the answer was wrong. The search was fine. The order was wrong. So a second, slower reader skims all twenty properly and puts the best one first.

Now the part where the analogy breaks, and it matters. A librarian understands the books. The model understands nothing. It has no beliefs about what is in there — it has a compressed summary, and it is very good at spotting when two summaries look alike. Everything that worked today worked because of that, and everything that failed today failed because of that.

Two words you will meet constantly

A bi-encoder is what you have used all day: it reads the question on its own, reads each document on its own, and compares the two sets of numbers. Fast, because every document was encoded once in advance — but it never sees the question and the document together.

A cross-encoder reads the question and one document at the same time and scores that pair. Far more accurate, and far slower: nothing can be done in advance, so it has to run once per document. That is why you search with a bi-encoder to get 20 candidates, then rerank just those 20 with a cross-encoder.

You don't have to host the model yourself

You just ran a cross-encoder on your own machine. Every retrieval platform also sells this as an endpoint, so you send text and get scores back:

Pinecone — pc.inference.rerank(model="bge-reranker-v2-m3", ...). Cohere Rerank, Voyage and Jina are the same idea from different providers. bge-reranker-v2-m3 is a cross-encoder doing exactly what you just ran locally.

Two things that bite people the first time. The embedding model used with these outputs 384 numbers, so the index has to be created with dimension=384. And after you upsert — the word vector databases use for "insert, or update if this id already exists" — wait until the vector count is above zero before querying, because indexing is not instant.

Try it yourself: the apple problem

A good test of all this: ask "Tell me about Apple's products" over a pile of documents about both the fruit and the company.

You now know exactly why that example is hard. To a bi-encoder — the embedding model from Step 4 — a document about the fruit and one about the company are both "apple, a thing, described", and the fruit can easily outrank the company. A cross-encoder reads your question and the document together, which is what pulls them apart.

  4. Why is a cross-encoder too slow to use as your search, but fine for reordering twenty results?
    Show the answer

    It reads the question and the document together, so it has to run once per document — impossible across 10,000. The bi-encoder encodes every document once, in advance, and only the question at query time. So the cheap model narrows 10,000 to 20 and the expensive one sorts those 20.
