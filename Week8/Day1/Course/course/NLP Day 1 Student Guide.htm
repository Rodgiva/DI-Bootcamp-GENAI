Making text into something a computer can rank — and classify

You have ten restaurant reviews and one question somebody typed. By the end of today you will have built a working search engine that answers it — out of nothing but counting words, with no AI model anywhere in it. Then you reuse the same numbers twice more: to train a small model that learns words from their company, and to build a spam filter.

Everything in this section is covered in today's session.

By the end of today you can:

  1. Cut text into tokens, and say why .split() isn't good enough.
  2. Strip out stopwords and reduce words to their root form.
  3. Turn a document into a row of numbers with Bag of Words and TF-IDF.
  4. Rank documents against a question using cosine similarity.
  5. Explain exactly why that whole system collapses when the question is reworded.
  6. Label words with their part of speech and pull names out of raw text.
  7. Train a Word2Vec model, and say precisely why ten reviews aren't enough for it.
  8. Run the same tools over a whole book, and tune min_df / max_df.
  9. Train and read the scores of a spam classifier built on the same TF-IDF vectors.

Why it matters. This is the half of search that meaning-based AI is bad at — exact terms, error codes, product names. Real systems run both methods at once, and you cannot build that knowing only one half. The failure you hit at the end of today is what the rest of the week exists to fix.

Where today sits in the week

The one idea: a computer cannot do anything with a sentence. It can only do arithmetic. So everything today answers one question — how do you turn text into numbers? — and then shows two things you can do once you have them.

That is why the library list looks scattered. It is really three jobs in a line:

  1. Clean the text. NLTK — word_tokenize, stopwords, the lemmatizer, pos_tag — plus spaCy for names. Messy sentences become tidy word lists. Steps 5–8, 14–15.
  2. Turn words into numbers. scikit-learn: CountVectorizer counts them, TfidfVectorizer weights them so rare words count for more. Then gensim's Word2Vec, which is a different way of making numbers — and the bridge to tomorrow. Steps 9–10, 16.
  3. Use the numbers. cosine_similarity to rank documents against a question, and Naive Bayes to classify them. Same vectors, two jobs — that is the punchline of the day. Steps 11–13, 18.

What today is not. Today is word matching only. The search engine finds the sushi review because your question literally contains the word sushi. Ask for raw fish and every score is 0.000 — that is Step 12, and it is on purpose. Meaning-based search is tomorrow's job; today's last hour exists to make you want it.

And there is no evaluation today — no BLEU, no ROUGE, no benchmarking. The closest thing is reading the classifier's precision and recall in Step 18, and that is about one model, not about comparing systems. Evaluation is Day 4.

One honest wrinkle: Word2Vec in Step 16 is a meaning-based representation, so today does not end perfectly clean. That is deliberate — it is a preview, trained badly on sixty words, so you see the mechanism before tomorrow hands you a real one.

STEP 5

Break text into words

Every technique in this subject works on tokens, not sentences — so this is the step that turns text into something countable.

When you would want sentences

Think of a 40-page insurance policy. If you search it as one document, a hit tells you "the answer is somewhere in these 40 pages" — useless. Cut it into sentences first and a hit points at the one sentence that answers the question. Splitting long documents into smaller pieces before searching them is called chunking, and it is most of the work on Day 3. sent_tokenize is the simplest way to do it.

STEP 6

Throw away the filler words

Words like the, is and at appear everywhere and tell you nothing about which review is which. They're called stopwords, and removing them is what makes the numbers in Step 9 mean something.

Stopwords
stop_words = set(stopwords.words('english'))
print(f"English stopwords: {len(stop_words)}")
print(sorted(stop_words)[:15])

tokens = word_tokenize(REVIEWS[8].lower())
kept = [t for t in tokens if t not in stop_words]
print()
print("before:", tokens)
print("after :", kept)
What the code does
  1. set(stopwords.words('english')) — loads the list of 198 filler words. We wrap it in a set because checking membership in a set is near-instant, and we are about to do it thousands of times.
  2. word_tokenize(REVIEWS[8].lower()) — lowercases the review first, then cuts it into tokens — The and the have to match the stopword list, which is all lowercase.
  3. [t for t in tokens if t not in stop_words] — keeps every token that is not in the list. This one line is the whole of stopword removal.
  4. print before / after — prints both lists so you can see exactly which words disappeared.
Checkpoint
English stopwords: 198
['a', 'about', 'above', 'after', 'again', ...]

before: ['the', 'steakhouse', 'on', 'main', 'street', 'has', 'a', 'cozy', ...]
after : ['steakhouse', 'main', 'street', 'cozy', 'atmosphere', 'excellent', ...]
Notice the full stop is still there. Punctuation isn't a stopword — we remove it separately in Step 8.
Why throwing words away helps

It feels wrong to delete words. Here is why it works. Imagine a library where every single book has the word the in it. If you walk up to the desk and ask for books containing the, the librarian hands you the whole library — you have learned nothing.

Now ask for sushi. One shelf. A word is only useful for telling documents apart if it is missing from some of them. the, is and at are in all ten reviews, so they can never separate one review from another — they are pure weight. Removing them is not losing information; it is removing the part that carries none.

STEP 7

Cut words back to their root

Someone searching for “price” should find a review that says “prices”. There are two ways to make those match, and they are not equally good.

Stemming vs lemmatization
stemmer = PorterStemmer()
lemmatizer = WordNetLemmatizer()

words = ["prices", "dishes", "steaks", "options",
         "traveled", "loved", "friendly", "amazing"]

print(f"{'word':<12}{'stemmed':<12}{'lemma':<12}{'lemma as verb'}")
print("-" * 52)
for w in words:
    print(f"{w:<12}{stemmer.stem(w):<12}"
          f"{lemmatizer.lemmatize(w):<12}{lemmatizer.lemmatize(w, pos='v')}")
What the code does
  1. PorterStemmer() / WordNetLemmatizer() — creates one of each tool. They solve the same problem in two different ways.
  2. words = [...] — eight words taken from our actual reviews, chosen because they behave differently.
  3. stemmer.stem(w) — chops letters off the end by rule. Fast, and it does not care whether the result is a real word.
  4. lemmatizer.lemmatize(w) — looks the word up in a dictionary and returns its base form. Assumes the word is a noun.
  5. lemmatizer.lemmatize(w, pos='v') — the same lookup, but telling it the word is a verb. Compare the last two columns — the answer changes.
  6. print(f"{w:<12}...") — the :<12 just pads each column to twelve characters so the table lines up.
Checkpoint
word        stemmed     lemma       lemma as verb
----------------------------------------------------
prices      price       price       price
dishes      dish        dish        dish
...
friendly    friendli    friendly    friendly
amazing     amaz        amazing     amaze
traveled    travel      traveled    travel
Look at friendli and amaz. Those aren't words. Stemming chops letters off by rule. Lemmatization looks the word up and gives you a real one — which is why we use it.
In plain words

A lemma is the form you'd look up in a dictionary. The lemma of "prices" is "price". That's the whole idea.

Which one should you use?

Both of them exist to make prices and price count as the same word. The difference is what they leave behind.

Stemming chops by rule and does not care if the result is a real word: amazing → amaz, friendly → friendli. That is fine if a machine is the only thing that ever reads it — amaz matches amaz perfectly well. It is useless the moment you want to show a user what their search matched, or print a list of top words.

Lemmatization looks the word up in a dictionary and returns something real: amazing → amazing, traveled → travel. Slower, and it needs the dictionary downloaded, which is what Step 2 was doing. We use lemmatization all day, because the word clouds and top-word lists later only make sense with real words in them.

STEP 8

Put it together into one function

Steps 5 to 7 in one reusable function, so every piece of text gets exactly the same treatment. This is the one piece of code today worth reading line by line.

The cleaning pipeline
def preprocess_text(text):
    text = text.lower()                                             # 1 lowercase
    tokens = word_tokenize(text)                                    # 2 split into words
    tokens = [t for t in tokens if t not in string.punctuation]     # 3 drop punctuation
    tokens = [t for t in tokens if t not in stop_words]             # 4 drop stopwords
    tokens = [lemmatizer.lemmatize(t) for t in tokens]              # 5 back to root form
    return ' '.join(tokens)

clean_reviews = [preprocess_text(r) for r in REVIEWS]

for i, (raw, clean) in enumerate(zip(REVIEWS, clean_reviews)):
    print(f"[{i}] {clean}")
What the code does
  1. text.lower() — step 1 — everything lowercase, so Sushi and sushi count as the same word.
  2. word_tokenize(text) — step 2 — cut into tokens, as in Step 5.
  3. if t not in string.punctuation — step 3 — drop any token that is a single punctuation character.
  4. if t not in stop_words — step 4 — drop the filler words, as in Step 6.
  5. lemmatizer.lemmatize(t) — step 5 — cut each remaining word back to its dictionary form, as in Step 7.
  6. ' '.join(tokens) — glues the surviving tokens back into one string, because that is what Steps 9 to 12 expect.
  7. [preprocess_text(r) for r in REVIEWS] — runs the whole function over all ten reviews and keeps the results in clean_reviews.
Checkpoint
[0] mcdonald 's food ok service bad
[1] would n't recommend japanese restaurant anyone
[2] loved restaurant traveled thailand last summer
...
[7] sushi sushi express always fresh price reasonable
Row 7 is the one to look at: prices became price. Our question says "prices" too — in Step 11 that's what makes them match.
Two odd things you'll spot — both real

's survives in rows 0 and 4, because it's two characters and our punctuation filter only catches single ones. And row 1 keeps n't — which accidentally saves the word "not" from being deleted as a stopword.

Cleaning text throws information away. Deciding what to throw away is a real design choice, and for some jobs deleting "not" would be a serious bug.

STEP 10

Weight the useful words higher

Needs mini_docs from Step 9. Counting treats every word as equally important. TF-IDF fixes that: a word that appears in every document tells you nothing, so it gets pushed down.

TF-IDF
tv = TfidfVectorizer()
tfidf_mini = tv.fit_transform(mini_docs)

print(pd.DataFrame(tfidf_mini.toarray().round(2),
                   columns=tv.get_feature_names_out(),
                   index=["Doc1", "Doc2", "Doc3"]))
What the code does
  1. TfidfVectorizer() — the same idea as CountVectorizer, but it weights each count instead of just counting.
  2. tv.fit_transform(mini_docs) — same two jobs: build the vocabulary, then produce the weighted numbers.
  3. .round(2) — rounds to two decimals purely so the table is readable — the real numbers have many more digits.

Doc1     0.00   0.00   0.53   0.00    0.53        0.53     0.00  0.00   0.40
Doc2     0.00   0.56   0.00   0.43    0.00        0.00     0.43  0.56   0.00

Compare Doc1 with Step 9. sushi scores 0.40 but fresh scores 0.53 — because "sushi" also appears in Doc3, while "fresh" is unique to Doc1. Rarer word, higher score.
In plain words

TF — term frequency — asks "how much is this document about the word?" Just count it: a review saying sushi twice is more about sushi than one saying it once.

IDF — inverse document frequency — asks "does this word tell us anything at all?" It looks at how many documents contain the word and gives a high score to rare words, a low one to common ones.

Multiply them, and here it is worked through on the table above. fresh is in Doc1 only → rare → high IDF → final score 0.53. sushi is in Doc1 and Doc3 → less rare → lower IDF → final score 0.40. Both appear exactly once in Doc1. They score differently purely because of how the rest of the collection uses them.

Everyday version: if a friend says "I went to a restaurant", you have learned almost nothing — everyone goes to restaurants. If they say "I went to Nobu", that single word tells you a great deal. Rare words carry information, common words carry almost none, and TF-IDF is that idea written as arithmetic.

Why amazing scores 0.60 and slow 0.56

Both appear in exactly one document, so they are equally rare — before scaling, both are 1.6931.

Then each row is divided by its own length, so a long document cannot win just by being long. Doc2 has two rare words sharing the row; Doc3 has one. So amazing keeps more of it.

STEP 11

Answer the question

The payoff: a working search engine with no AI model of any kind — and the baseline every other day this week is measured against.

Search
tfidf = TfidfVectorizer()
doc_vectors = tfidf.fit_transform(clean_reviews)

clean_query  = preprocess_text(QUERY)          # the SAME cleaning as the reviews
query_vector = tfidf.transform([clean_query])  # transform, NOT fit_transform

scores = cosine_similarity(query_vector, doc_vectors)[0]

print(f"QUERY:   '{QUERY}'")
print(f"CLEANED: '{clean_query}'\n")
for i in np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:5]:
    print(f"{scores[i]:.3f}  [{i}] {REVIEWS[i]}")
What the code does
  1. TfidfVectorizer().fit_transform(clean_reviews) — builds the vocabulary from the cleaned reviews and turns all ten into rows of numbers.
  2. preprocess_text(QUERY) — puts the question through exactly the same cleaning as the reviews. If you skip this, the question's words will not match.
  3. tfidf.transform([clean_query]) — turns the question into a row of numbers using the vocabulary already built. transform, never fit_transform — see the warning below.
  4. cosine_similarity(query_vector, doc_vectors)[0] — compares the question's row against all ten review rows and returns ten scores between 0 and 1.
  5. np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:5] — sorts the scores lowest-to-highest, reverses that, and takes the first five — so, the five best-matching reviews.
Checkpoint

0.148  [9] The dessert selection at Sweet Treats is amazing and the prices are fair.

The one line that matters most

The question goes through transform, never fit_transform. The documents and the question have to be measured with the same ruler.

fit_transform here would throw the ruler away and build a new one from the question alone — 4 words instead of 58 — and cosine_similarity then crashes with a dimension mismatch. The quiet version of this mistake is fitting on everything at once (documents and question, or train and test): the shapes match, nothing errors, and the score is just wrong. That is the trap in Step 18.

What cosine similarity actually measures

Every review is now a row of numbers, and so is the question. Cosine similarity compares two such rows and returns one number between 0 and 1. 1 means they point in exactly the same direction; 0 means they have nothing in common.

The useful part is what it ignores: length. Think of two shopping lists. One says "2 apples, 1 bread". The other says "20 apples, 10 bread". Those are very different sizes, but they describe the same kind of shopping — and cosine similarity scores them as near-identical, because it looks at the proportions, not the totals.

That is exactly what you want here. Our question is four words long and review [7] is twelve words long. A measure based on size would say they are miles apart. Cosine similarity says 0.829, because what they contain lines up.

The point of running both experiments

Step 12 and Step 13 are the same code on the same data. Asked in someone's own words, it scores zero. Asked for an exact name, it is exactly right. So neither way of searching wins on its own — which is why real systems run word matching and meaning matching side by side and merge the two rankings. That combination is called hybrid search, and today you built one of its two halves.

What production actually runs: BM25

Everything you built today is real, but no search engine ships plain TF-IDF. They ship a tuned version of the same idea called BM25, and it fixes two things.

One: repetition stops counting after a while. In TF-IDF, a review that says pizza ten times scores ten times higher on pizza than one that says it once. That is not how relevance works — a page that mentions pizza ten times is not ten times more about pizza than a page that mentions it once. BM25 lets the score rise quickly for the first few mentions and then flatten out. Mention three and four barely move it; mention fifty moves it almost not at all.

Two: long documents stop winning by being long. A 5,000-word page contains more of every word than a 50-word page, so under plain counting it looks more relevant to everything. BM25 divides by how long the document is, so a short review that is entirely about sushi can beat a long one that mentions sushi in passing.

You do not need to implement it. If you have heard of Elasticsearch, OpenSearch or Azure AI Search, BM25 is what they rank with — and the whole of today is what it is built on top of. Knowing TF-IDF means you can read BM25's settings and know what they will do.

STEP 16

Word2Vec — learning what words mean

Step 12 failed because raw fish and sushi mean the same thing but don't look the same. The fix is to give every word a short list of numbers that stands for its meaning. Word2Vec (2013) is where that idea started, and it is small enough to train on our ten reviews in about two seconds. It will not work well — and seeing exactly how it fails tells you what the real thing needs.

Prepare the input

Train it, then look at the neighbours

    sentences=tokenized_reviews,
    vector_size=50,   # length of each word's list of numbers
    window=3,         # how many words either side count as "context"
    min_count=1,      # keep every word (normally 5+)
    sg=1,             # skip-gram
    epochs=100,
    seed=42, workers=1,
)

print("First 8 numbers for 'sushi':")
print(w2v.wv['sushi'][:8].round(3))

    print(f"  {w:12} {score:.3f}")
What the code does
  1. vector_size=50 — each word gets 50 numbers. Real models use 100–300.
  2. window=3 — a word's "company" is the three words either side. That is the entire idea behind Word2Vec: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. You have probably never seen scalpel defined, but you know roughly what one is, because it turns up near surgeon, incision and sterile. The model learns the same way, from nothing but which words sit near which.
  3. sg=1 — skip-gram: show the model one word, make it guess the words around it. Better on tiny data than the alternative (sg=0, CBOW, which guesses the missing middle word).
  4. epochs=100 — read the corpus 100 times. Normally far fewer; ten sentences give it almost nothing to learn from.
  5. seed=42, workers=1 — makes the result repeatable, so your numbers match the checkpoint.
  6. most_similar — the five words whose number-lists point in the most similar direction.

Why this failure is the useful part

A word's meaning comes from the variety of company it keeps, and two occurrences in one sentence is one context. A real model sees sushi in millions of sentences — near fish, raw, rice — and what survives across all of them is the meaning. That is tomorrow.

And chapter 1 is key, eat, dinah, fell, table, hall — the rabbit-hole chapter, described by a computer that has never read the book. Same text as the frequency list above; the only difference is weighting.
Why max_df is the interesting one

We never gave it a stopword list for this book. We said "throw away anything that appears in more than half the chapters" and it worked out the filler for itself. That is how you handle a corpus whose filler words are not English filler words at all — legal documents, medical notes, support tickets, where every document contains patient or agreement.

STEP 18

Classify, don't just search

Every step so far asked which document matches this question? This one asks a different question: which category does this document belong to? Spam or not. Positive or negative. Urgent or routine. You already have everything you need — the same TF-IDF vectors, plus about four lines.

Train a spam classifier
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, classification_report

MESSAGES = [
    ("spam", "WINNER! You have won a free iPhone. Click here to claim now"),
    ("spam", "Congratulations, you are selected for a free holiday. Reply WIN to claim"),
    ("spam", "URGENT: your account will be closed. Verify your details at this link"),
    ("spam", "Free entry into our weekly prize draw. Text YES now"),
    ("spam", "You have won 1000 dollars cash. Claim your prize today"),
    ("spam", "Cheap meds online, no prescription needed, order now"),
    ("spam", "Click this link to claim your free gift card immediately"),
    ("spam", "Final notice: claim your refund now or lose it forever"),
    ("spam", "Earn money fast working from home, no experience needed"),
    ("spam", "Your package is held. Pay the fee at this link to release it"),
    ("spam", "Exclusive offer just for you, 90 percent off, today only"),
    ("spam", "You are a lucky winner, call this number to collect your prize"),
    ("ham",  "Are we still meeting at six tonight"),
    ("ham",  "I left the keys on the kitchen table"),
    ("ham",  "Can you send me the slides before the lecture please"),
    ("ham",  "Running ten minutes late, sorry, see you soon"),
    ("ham",  "Mum called, she wants to know about Sunday lunch"),
    ("ham",  "The train is delayed again, I will let you know"),
    ("ham",  "Happy birthday! Hope you have a lovely day"),
    ("ham",  "Did you finish the homework for tomorrow"),
    ("ham",  "Let us grab coffee after the meeting"),
    ("ham",  "I have booked the table for eight o clock"),
    ("ham",  "Thanks for your help yesterday, it made a big difference"),
    ("ham",  "The report is attached, let me know what you think"),
]
labels = [label for label, _ in MESSAGES]
texts  = [text  for _, text  in MESSAGES]

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    texts, labels, test_size=0.25, random_state=42, stratify=labels)

vec = TfidfVectorizer()
clf = MultinomialNB().fit(vec.fit_transform(X_train), y_train)
predictions = clf.predict(vec.transform(X_test))

print(f"Trained on {len(X_train)} messages, tested on {len(X_test)}")
print(f"Accuracy: {accuracy_score(y_test, predictions):.3f}\n")
print(classification_report(y_test, predictions))
What the code does
  1. MESSAGES — 24 labelled examples, 12 of each. ham is the standard name for "not spam" — it has been called that since the 1990s and you will see it in every spam dataset. The labels are the whole difference between searching and classifying: somebody had to write them by hand.
  2. train_test_split(..., test_size=0.25, stratify=labels) — holds back a quarter of the messages so the model is scored on messages it has never seen. stratify keeps the spam/ham balance in both halves.
  3. vec.fit_transform(X_train) then vec.transform(X_test) — the Step 11 rule again. Fit on the training messages only; transform the test ones with the result. Fit on everything and the model has already seen the test set.
  4. MultinomialNB() — Naive Bayes. For each word it asks how much more often that word shows up in spam than in ham, then multiplies those up. The standard first classifier for text.
  5. classification_report — precision, recall and F1 per class, not one overall number.
Checkpoint
Trained on 18 messages, tested on 6
Accuracy: 0.667

              precision    recall  f1-score   support

         ham       1.00      0.33      0.50         3
        spam       0.60      1.00      0.75         3

Three new words in that table — precision, recall and F1. They are explained in plain English just below; read that first if you need to, then come back. Read the rows, not the accuracy. Spam recall 1.00 — it caught every spam message. Ham recall 0.33 — it called two of the three normal messages spam. In a real mail system that is a disaster: your colleague's message about the meeting lands in the junk folder. The single number 0.667 told you none of that.
Try it on new messages
for message in ["Win a free iPhone now!",
                "Can you pick up milk on the way home"]:
    probabilities = clf.predict_proba(vec.transform([message]))[0]
    scores = {str(label): round(float(p), 3) for label, p in zip(clf.classes_, probabilities)}
    print(f"{message!r}\n  → {clf.predict(vec.transform([message]))[0]}   {scores}\n")
What the code does
  1. vec.transform([message]) — the new message through the same vectorizer. Same rule as Step 11, third time today.
  2. predict_proba — not just the label but how confident the model is. Always look at this before trusting a prediction.

Both right — and both uncomfortably close to a coin flip. 0.702 and 0.612 are not the numbers of a model you would deploy. The fix is not a cleverer algorithm; it is a few thousand more labelled messages.
Two jobs, one representation

TF-IDF turned text into numbers this morning so you could rank documents. The same numbers, with labels attached, let you classify them. That is why the boring part was worth two hours. Swapping MultinomialNB() for LogisticRegression() is one line and usually a little better; everything around it stays identical.

What Naive Bayes does

It keeps a tally. free turned up in four spam messages and no normal ones, so free counts towards spam. meeting counts against. Every word gets a weight, and a new message multiplies them up.

"Naive" means it assumes the words are independent. They aren't. It works anyway.

Precision and recall

Think of a net. Recall — of all the spam out there, how much did we catch? 1.00, everything. Precision — of what we caught, how much really was spam? 0.60, so four in ten were innocent.

Catch everything and precision drops. Catch only the obvious one and recall drops. That is why accuracy alone tells you nothing.
